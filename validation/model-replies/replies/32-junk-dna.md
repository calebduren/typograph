# Is "junk DNA" really junk?

Short answer: the phrase was always more of a placeholder than a claim, and most of what it once described has turned out to do something.[^1]

## Where the term came from

In the 1970s, researchers noticed that only a small share of the human genome coded for proteins, about 1 to 2 percent. The rest seemed to have no obvious job. Susumu Ohno popularized the label "junk DNA," meaning sequence that we couldn't yet explain and that might be evolutionary leftovers.

## What we've learned since

- **Regulation.** Large parts of the "non-coding" genome switch genes on and off. Enhancers and silencers control when and where a gene is used.
- **Functional RNA.** Some non-coding DNA is transcribed into RNA molecules that do work themselves, such as ribosomal and transfer RNA.
- **Structure.** Repeated sequences at chromosome ends, the telomeres, protect them, and regions near the centromere hold the chromosome together during cell division.
- **Evolutionary raw material.** Duplicated and old sequence can be recruited for new roles over long timescales.

## The disagreement

How much is functional is disputed. The ENCODE project reported in 2012 that about 80 percent of the genome showed biochemical activity (Fig. 1 in the original paper), which was widely read as "most of it is functional." Many evolutionary biologists objected that biochemical activity isn't the same as biological function, and that a large share of the genome likely doesn't matter.

The main argument for a smaller estimate comes from genetic load. If most of the genome were under selection, mutations would be deleterious at a rate that a population couldn't survive. That points to a functional fraction of perhaps 10 to 15 percent, though the numbers depend on assumptions.

## A useful way to think about it

There are three categories, not two:

1. Sequences that clearly do something.
2. Sequences that clearly don't, like some broken copies of old viruses.
3. A large middle category we haven't sorted out.

Calling everything in category 3 "junk" is a mistake, but so is calling everything "functional."

## Bottom line

"Junk" is a poor term for what we now know, because much of the genome is neither essential nor useless.[^2] Expect the estimate to keep shifting as methods improve.

[^1]: Ohno's 1972 paper is the usual citation, though earlier authors discussed the idea.

[^2]: Some non-functional DNA still matters to evolution, because it can change over time without harming the organism.
