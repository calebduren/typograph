# Tracing an Irish surname: O'Brien, and the spelling problem

Irish family research is rewarding, but the records were written by people who spelled names the way they heard them. That's the first thing to plan for.

## Understand the name

"O'Brien" comes from the Irish Ó Briain, meaning "descendant of Brian." The apostrophe was introduced when the name was anglicized, and it stands in for the missing letters. You'll find the same name as O'Brian, Brian, Bryan, and even Obrien in different documents. Search all of them.

The prefix "Mac" or "Mc" works differently, and "Fitz" comes from Norman French. Each has its own history, so don't assume a name is Irish just because it looks it.

## Where to look

1. **Civil records.** Civil registration in Ireland began in 1864 (1845 for non-Catholic marriages). The index is searchable online.
2. **Church registers.** Parish registers go back further, sometimes to the late 1700s, but coverage is patchy.
3. **Census returns.** The 1901 and 1911 censuses survive in full and give exact ages and relatives.
4. **Land records.** Griffith's Valuation, from the 1850s, lists occupiers by townland.

## A tip about wording

When you contact a record office, be clear about which relatives you mean. "My great-grandmother's sister's husband" gets lost. The Smiths' house on Elm Road in one record might be the O'Briens' house in the next, because a daughter married in. Draw a small tree before you write.

## Cautions

- Don't trust family stories about a "castle" or a "coat of arms." Coats of arms belonged to individuals, not surnames.
- DNA tests can confirm a cluster but rarely pinpoint a townland.
- Emigration records are often more complete than the Irish side, so try Ellis Island and Liverpool passenger lists.
