# What the Gettysburg Address is doing

Lincoln's speech runs about 270 words, and almost every phrase is doing work. It's worth reading slowly.

## The structure

The address falls into three movements. The first looks back, the second looks at the present, and the third looks ahead. You can hear the shift in tense.

> Four score and seven years ago our fathers brought forth on this continent, a new nation, conceived in Liberty, and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal.

Two things stand out in this sentence. First, "four score and seven" reaches past 1776 to the Declaration, moving the founding earlier than the Constitution. Second, the word "proposition" frames the nation as an idea that can be tested, and the war as that test.

## The pivot

The middle section shifts to the audience. Lincoln says the ground cannot be consecrated by words, because the soldiers have already done it. It's a strikingly humble claim for a man who was speaking at a ceremony that was, on paper, about consecrating the ground.

> The world will little note, nor long remember what we say here, but it can never forget what they did here.

History has, of course, remembered both. People often quote that line as an irony, but it's also a plain statement of priority: action over speech.

## The close

The final sentence gives the audience an assignment. They aren't asked to grieve; they're asked to finish the work, and to see that "government of the people, by the people, for the people, shall not perish from the earth."

## Why it works

1. **Brevity.** The main speaker that day, Edward Everett, spoke for about two hours. Lincoln spoke for about two minutes.
2. **Repetition.** "Here" appears repeatedly, anchoring the abstract argument in a physical place.
3. **Religious cadence.** The King James Bible was the shared idiom of the audience.
4. **A verbal turn.** Lincoln moves from "we" to "us" to "the living," making every listener part of the sentence.

## Reading it yourself

The text is in the public domain, and the Library of Congress hosts images of Lincoln's drafts at https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/gettysburg-address/. Comparing the drafts shows how he tightened the language, including the addition of "under God" in later copies.

If you're teaching it, I can suggest a five-minute close-reading exercise that works well for a classroom.
