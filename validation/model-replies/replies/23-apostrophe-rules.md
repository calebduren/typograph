# Apostrophes: the rules that cover 95% of cases

Apostrophes do just two jobs: they mark missing letters, and they mark possession. They never make a plural. Once you separate the two jobs, the rest is bookkeeping.

## Contractions

An apostrophe replaces the missing letters: it's (it is), don't (do not), we're (we are), they'd (they would or they had), and who's (who is). If you can expand the word to two words and it still makes sense, the apostrophe belongs.

## Possession

- **Singular nouns:** add 's. The dog's bowl. James's book (many style guides prefer this over "James' book").
- **Plural nouns ending in s:** add just the apostrophe. The Smiths' house, the students' grades, the cats' toys.
- **Irregular plurals:** add 's. The children's room, the people's choice.
- **Pronouns:** no apostrophe at all. Its, whose, hers, ours, theirs, yours.

## The "its" problem

"It's" always means "it is" or "it has." The possessive is "its," with no apostrophe. Test by substituting "it is": if the sentence still works, use the apostrophe.

## Plurals

Don't use an apostrophe for a plain plural: "two CDs," "the 1990s," "several PhDs." A few writers add one for clarity in cases like "dot the i's and cross the t's," where the plain form would look strange.

## Numbers and dates

- The decade is the 1990s or the '90s, with the apostrophe showing dropped digits.
- Possessives work as usual: 2025's results, Q3's numbers.

## Leading apostrophes

Words that drop their first letters, such as 'em, 'til, and 'cause, take an apostrophe at the start. Word processors often turn this into an opening single quote by mistake, which is a well-known typography error.

## Quick reference

| Wrong                    | Right                   |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------- |
| The dog wagged it's tail | The dog wagged its tail |
| Your welcome             | You're welcome          |
| Apple's are on sale      | Apples are on sale      |
| The Jones's house        | The Joneses' house      |

## Exceptions and style

Style guides differ on things like "James's" versus "James'." Choose one rule, and apply it consistently across a document. Consistency reads as care; mixing styles reads as carelessness.
