# Building a simple household budget

Most budgets fail because they're too detailed to maintain. A useful one has few categories and a fixed review time.

## Start with the numbers

Take your monthly take-home pay, say $4,800, and split it into three buckets:

| Bucket                                   | Share | Amount |
| ---------------------------------------- | ----- | ------ |
| Needs (rent, food, insurance, transport) | 50%   | $2,400 |
| Wants                                    | 30%   | $1,440 |
| Saving and debt                          | 20%   | $960   |

This 50/30/20 split is a starting point, not a rule. In an expensive city, needs may take 60% or more. If that's you, don't feel like you've failed; adjust the other buckets.

## Track for one month

Before you set limits, see where money really goes. Download the last three months of statements and tag each line. Most people find $100 to $300 a month in subscriptions and small purchases they'd forgotten about.

## Automate what you can

1. Set up a transfer to savings the day after payday. If you never see it, you won't spend it.
2. Schedule bill payments for just after your paycheck lands.
3. Use a separate account for irregular expenses like car repairs and annual fees. Put $50 to $100 a month in it.

## Emergency fund

The standard target is three to six months of essential expenses. If your needs are $2,400 a month, that's $7,200 to $14,400. Start with a smaller goal, such as $1,000, so that a broken phone doesn't go on a credit card.

## Debt

List balances and interest rates. Pay the minimum on everything, and put extra toward the highest rate first (the "avalanche" method) or the smallest balance first (the "snowball" method). The avalanche saves more money; the snowball is easier to stick with. Pick the one you'll actually follow.

## Review

Set a 20 min slot once a month. Check three things: did you stay within each bucket, did anything unusual come up, and is a bill about to increase?

I'm not a financial advisor, and I don't know your situation. For decisions about investing, taxes, or large debts, talk to a professional who can look at the full picture.
