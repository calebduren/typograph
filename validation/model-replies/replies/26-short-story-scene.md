Here's a short scene, about 350 words, with a lighthouse keeper and an unexpected visitor. I kept the dialogue spare and let the setting do some of the talking.

---

## The Last Light

The boat came in at dusk, which nobody ever did.

"You're Ellis?" the woman called from the dock. She held her hat against the wind with one hand and a leather case with the other.

"Depends who's asking."

"Harriet Vane. The Board sent me." She climbed the steps like someone who'd rehearsed the words on the crossing. "I'm to tell you the light is being automated in the spring."

Ellis said nothing. He turned and went up the path, and she followed him inside, where the kettle was already ticking on the stove.

"Thirty-one years," he said at last. "I've kept it thirty-one years, and they send a girl with a case to tell me."

"I'm not a girl, Mr. Ellis. I'm an engineer."

"Then you'll know how it works."

"I do." She set the case on the table. "It's a good machine. It'll run without anyone here, and it won't need to sleep.

"But I read your logs on the way out. Every night for thirty-one years, the same three lines. Time lit, weather, and the name of any ship that passed. That's not a duty; that's a habit of paying attention. I wanted to meet the person who wrote them."

He poured two cups, slowly.

"And what do you make of him?"

"That he's going to tell me the machine can't do what he does."

"No." Ellis almost smiled. "It'll do it fine. It'll do everything but notice the ones that don't pass."

She waited.

"There was a fishing boat once, in '94," he said. "Its light was out, and it drifted past us at three in the morning. Nobody was watching for it but me. I called the coast guard, and they found it on the rocks by dawn. All hands were saved."

"So what should I write in my report?"

He looked out the window, where the beam had begun its long, steady sweep across the water.

"Write that somebody ought to keep watch," he said. "Then let them decide."
