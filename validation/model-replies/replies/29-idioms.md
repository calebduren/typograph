# Idioms that trip up English learners

Idioms are hard because you can't work out the meaning from the words. Here are ones I'd prioritize, sorted by how often you'll hear them.

## Everyday phrases

| Idiom                   | Meaning                 | Example                              |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| "Break the ice"         | Make people comfortable | "A joke helped break the ice."       |
| "Under the weather"     | Slightly ill            | "I'm feeling under the weather."     |
| "Piece of cake"         | Very easy               | "The test was a piece of cake."      |
| "Hit the road"          | Leave                   | "We should hit the road by nine."    |
| "Cost an arm and a leg" | Be very expensive       | "That watch costs an arm and a leg." |

## Work idioms

- **"Touch base":** make brief contact. "Let's touch base on Friday."
- **"On the same page":** in agreement. "I want to be sure we're on the same page."
- **"Drop the ball":** fail to do something. "Someone dropped the ball on the invoice."
- **"Get the ball rolling":** start. "Let's get the ball rolling with introductions."

## Informal speech

Spoken English shortens words in ways that don't appear in textbooks. You'll hear "gonna" (going to), "wanna" (want to), "lemme" (let me), and "y'all" (you all). In writing, you may also see 'cause for "because," 'em for "them," and 'til for "until."

Example: "Give 'em a minute; they'll be here 'til five 'cause the meeting's running late."

You don't need to use these, but you'll understand native speakers much better if you recognize them.

## Tips for learning

1. **Learn idioms in context.** A sentence beats a definition.
2. **Learn in small groups.** Five a week is enough.
3. **Watch out for literal readings.** "It's raining cats and dogs" isn't about animals, and your friends won't be offended if you ask what it means.
4. **Don't overuse them.** One well-chosen idiom sounds natural; five in a paragraph sounds forced.

## Regional differences

American, British, and Australian English have different idioms. "Bob's your uncle" is British; "the whole nine yards" is more American. If you're preparing for one region, focus there.
